Under the hood

The simulation and the light-to-pixel shading are written in Rust and compiled to a WebAssembly module of about 70 KB, embedded in this page. It has no imports and no bindings layer: JavaScript calls a handful of exported functions and reads the pixels and readings straight out of the module’s memory. JavaScript still handles the controls, the canvas drawing and the spectrum chart.

Inside the module, all six fields (temperature, fuel, oxygen, soot and both velocity components) ride the same flow, so each cell is traced back once and all six are sampled there. Diffusion is solved implicitly with temperature-dependent coefficients: thermal diffusivity and viscosity rise as T1.7, from 22 and 16 mm²/s in room air to about 400 and 300 in the flame, fuel vapour diffuses three times slower than heat, and the wax is insulating. Each field takes one tridiagonal sweep along x and one along y per substep, so there is nothing to iterate and nothing to converge. The pressure solve uses red-black Gauss–Seidel, so no cell waits on the neighbour it just updated. The tone curve is a lookup table. The Engine panel shows what each stage costs on your device.

Simplifications: the grid is a 2D slice, so a mixing term stands in for the air a round flame draws in from the third dimension. Chemistry is a single global step, the gas is treated as optically thin, and transport follows kinetic theory with fixed Lewis and Prandtl numbers; the one-step reaction constants are tuned to give a candle-sized flame (about 18 mm tall, 7 mm wide) with the adiabatic flame temperature of a hydrocarbon in air. Temperatures, wavelengths and ratios in the readings are computed from the simulation each frame.
