Blackbody Candle

A candle you can light, where nothing about the flame is hand-drawn. Its shape comes from a small reacting, buoyant gas simulation, and every colour comes from Planck’s law seen through the CIE 1931 eye. The idea of building a flame rather than drawing one comes from Carmen Ansio’s A flame needs three sine waves; this one swaps the sine waves for physics.

Under the hood

The window is 42 × 70 mm of air on a grid of 0.25 mm cells. Every 1/240 s the engine applies buoyancy, a wandering room wind and the vortices they shed; a pressure projection with the thermal expansion of burning gas; semi-Lagrangian advection of temperature, fuel vapour, oxygen, soot and velocity; diffusion with coefficients that rise with temperature, as a gas’s do; and one-step chemistry with soot that forms where fuel is hot and burns away where oxygen and OH reach it, radiating as it goes. The wick is a curled hot surface that feeds wax vapour at a rate set by the heat it gets back from the flame, with the small fluctuations of a real capillary feed; a hard enough puff cools it past relighting, and the candle goes out.

Light is computed per cell from Planck’s law with soot’s 1/λ emissivity, integrated against the CIE 1931 observer, plus CH* and C₂* band light for the blue base. The camera sees that through the whole depth of a round flame, so the slice is projected along the line of sight, white-balanced to a blackbody of your choice, and tone-mapped per device pixel on the GPU. The readings are computed from the simulation each frame.

Simplifications: the grid is a 2D slice, so a mixing term stands in for the air a round flame draws in from the third dimension; chemistry is a single global step; the gas is optically thin; transport follows kinetic theory with fixed Lewis and Prandtl numbers; and the reaction constants are tuned to give a candle-sized flame with the adiabatic flame temperature of a hydrocarbon in air. Trends are physical, absolute numbers are not predictions.
